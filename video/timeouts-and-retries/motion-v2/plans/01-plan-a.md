# Lesson 01 — Wait for a response

## Teaching intent

Teach one mechanism: asking a remote service for CI status takes time, and an attempt timeout limits how long the caller waits for that response. First let the viewer follow a complete request and response. Then show a separate illustrative case in which the caller reaches its waiting limit. The response is evidence of the reported CI status; sending the request is not that evidence. A timeout is a local waiting outcome, not a failed-build result.

This is a plan only. Target roughly 42 seconds, with timing subordinate to comprehension. No narration, caption paragraphs, music bed, or physical-world metaphor. Do not introduce retries, backoff, overall operation deadlines, cancellation propagation, late response handling, or idempotency. The two cases are independent demonstrations separated by a complete stage fade, not successive attempts by the same operation.

## Software-native stage

Use a minimal request inspector, not a generic server-rack diagram. One large caller panel sits above one service panel; between them, two generously separated network tracks make the outgoing request and returning response distinguishable. The caller contains a pending result field and its own bounded wait rail. The service contains a single processing slot. This makes the timing rule visibly belong to the caller.

Use restrained dotted strokes and direction through packet movement, not marching background decoration. “Network” sits at (540, 1040), clear of both tracks. Amber means waiting or timeout; teal plus check means a received success response. Remote processing uses a white slot and one restrained amber sweep. Color is reinforced by shape and position.

## Time and motion contract

During each live attempt, two playback seconds represent one illustrative second. The normal case runs from playback 2 to 10 seconds: one illustrative second outbound, two processing, one inbound, totaling four. Its five-second limit would be reached at playback 12, but the wait rail stops when the response arrives at 10. The slow case starts at 19 and reaches its five-second limit at 29. Keep this mapping linear and identical in both cases. The longer holds occur after state transitions; they do not change recorded durations. The title or accompanying lesson copy should explain that values are illustrative, with no extra on-screen explanatory sentence.

Use linear rail progress and mostly linear packet travel with only short easing near endpoints. Waiting is represented by the steadily advancing caller rail, not a rapidly spinning clock. Service activity uses a busy sweep rather than a percentage-complete bar, because the caller cannot infer completion time from it. Let the interval after each major arrival or stop breathe for approximately 1.5–2.5 seconds; continuous elapsed-time motion is permitted during a live wait. Do not freeze a live timer to create a reading hold.

## Sound design

Actor identity is stable across cases: caller C-family notes; service E-family notes. Sends are an octave above arrivals and approximately 6 dB quieter. The network has no autonomous voice: motion represents transit between those actors. Pan send and arrival cues to the corresponding port, subtly enough for mono phone playback. Use sine mallets with a quiet 80 ms echo, limited repeats, and soft attacks. Processing cues should be low and sparse, not a typewriter stream. Fixed timer ticks belong to the caller and remain softer than service activity; omit a tick when it would mask a causal event. The timeout is a soft local stop with a downward interval, not an alarm. No success chime plays when the service merely finishes preparing a response. Sound supports events already visible and must never supply otherwise missing information.
